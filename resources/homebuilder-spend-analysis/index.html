How Does a Homebuilder Run a Spend Analysis?

By Hunter Caudle, Co-Founder and President · November 24, 2026

A homebuilder runs a spend analysis by pulling purchase, invoice and contract data from every division, cleaning supplier and item names, classifying each purchase into one set of cost codes, and organizing it by supplier, category, division and period. The result shows where the builder buys off contract, where divisions pay different prices for the same item, and which categories deserve attention first.

Hunter Caudle, co-founder and president of Vosik, describes the starting point at a builder grown by acquisition:

“In my corporate world, we had a lot of site builders on different systems, and every one of them formatted things their own way. Even the ones on the same system used it differently.”

Hunter Caudle, Co-Founder and President, Vosik

Key facts at a glance

  • A spend analysis classifies every purchase by supplier, category, division and period, so a builder can see what it buys, from whom, and at what price.
  • The NAHB Chart of Accounts uses account 1430 as the control account for direct construction costs, which covers direct labor, materials, trade contractors and equipment rentals.
  • NAHB's direct construction cost ledger gives windows their own code, 3300, and exterior doors their own code, 4300, which makes a ready-made category list for builder spend.
  • The Construction Specifications Institute's MasterFormat groups doors and windows in Division 08, Openings, another standard a builder can map spend to.
  • In NAHB's Cost of Doing Business Study, builders' cost of sales, covering land and direct and indirect construction costs, was 79.3% of revenue in 2023.
  • A price difference between divisions for the same item is a question to investigate, not proof of overpaying; freight, specification, quantity and timing can all explain it.
  • A spend analysis is only as reliable as the supplier names, item descriptions and units of measure behind it, which is why cleaning the data is the largest part of the work.

What is a spend analysis for a homebuilder?

A spend analysis for a homebuilder is a structured review of everything the company bought over a period, organized by supplier, cost category, division and time, and connected to the agreements that were supposed to govern those purchases. It answers four questions: what did we buy, from whom, at what price, and under which terms?

For a builder, the analysis matters because cost of sales is most of revenue. NAHB's Cost of Doing Business Study put builders' cost of sales at 79.3% of revenue in 2023. A spend analysis is how a builder finds which part of that cost purchasing can influence, and where to start.

What data does a homebuilder need?

A homebuilder needs purchase order, invoice and payment data from every division, plus the supplier agreements, price sheets and rebate terms that apply to them. Closings by plan let the analysis tie spend back to homes. Start with twelve months, so seasonal swings and price changes show up.

DataWhere it usually livesWhat it adds
Purchase orders and VPOsEach division's purchasing or ERP systemWhat was ordered, for which home and plan, and what went over budget
Invoices and paymentsAccounts payableWhat was actually billed and paid
Supplier agreements and price sheetsContract files, shared drives, emailWhat should have been paid
Rebate agreementsCorporate purchasing, supplier portalsWhat should come back after payment
Closings by plan and communitySales or finance systemsHow spend relates to homes delivered
Item masterPurchasing or estimating systemItem descriptions, units and specifications

At a builder that has grown by acquisition, these sources are rarely in one place or one format. That is normal, and it is not a reason to wait.

How do you clean supplier and item data?

Cleaning supplier and item data means making the same supplier, the same item and the same unit look the same everywhere, so spend can be added up and compared. It is usually the largest part of a spend analysis, and the part most likely to be skipped.

  • Supplier names. Merge variants of the same supplier (abbreviations, typos, branch names) under one name, and link local distributors to the national manufacturer where the agreement sits.
  • Item descriptions. Match the same window or sliding door across divisions, even when each division describes it differently. Manufacturer part numbers help where they exist.
  • Units of measure. Convert to one unit per item, so a price per unit means the same thing everywhere.
  • Specifications. Note size, series, glass package and options, so a premium sliding door is not compared with a standard one.
  • Credits and returns. Net them against the original purchase, not as separate spend.

How should a homebuilder classify its spend?

A homebuilder should classify spend into one set of cost categories used by every division, ideally one the builder already uses for job costing. Two published standards are a practical starting point: the NAHB Chart of Accounts, built for home builders, and the Construction Specifications Institute's MasterFormat.

StandardHow it groups windows and doorsBest fit
NAHB Chart of AccountsDirect construction cost ledger: 3300 Windows, 4300 Exterior doors, under control account 1430Builders who job-cost on NAHB codes
CSI MasterFormatDivision 08, Openings, covers doors and windowsBuilders whose estimates or specs follow MasterFormat
The builder's own cost codesWhatever the job-costing system already usesBuilders with consistent codes across divisions

The standard matters less than consistency. If one division books sliding doors under windows and another under exterior doors, category totals will be wrong in both. Map every division's codes to one list before comparing anything.

What is a spend cube, and how do you build one?

A spend cube is a table of spend organized along several dimensions at once, so the same data can be viewed by supplier, by category, by division or by period without rebuilding it. For a homebuilder, the useful dimensions are supplier, category, division, community or plan, period, and contract status.

DimensionExample valuesQuestion it answers
SupplierNational manufacturer, local distributorWho do we buy from, and how concentrated is it?
Category3300 Windows, 4300 Exterior doorsWhere does the money go?
DivisionEach operating divisionWhich divisions buy differently?
Plan or communityPlan names, community namesWhich homes cost more to build?
PeriodMonth, quarterHow are prices and volumes changing?
Contract statusOn national agreement, on local agreement, off contractHow much is bought under negotiated terms?

A spreadsheet pivot table is a spend cube in its simplest form. It works for one division. Across many divisions and suppliers, the cube gets too large, and the cleaning steps above have to be repeated every time new data arrives.

What should a homebuilder look for in the results?

A homebuilder should look for differences it can act on: spend outside agreements, different prices for the same item, suppliers that could be consolidated, and costs rising faster than expected. Each finding is a lead to investigate, not a conclusion.

FindingWhat it may meanWhat to check before acting
Spend off contractDivisions buying outside negotiated termsWhether a national or local agreement covers the item
Price spread for the same itemOne division paying moreSpecification, quantity, freight, geography and timing
Many suppliers in one categoryVolume split too thin for leverageCode, availability and service differences by region
Prices rising faster than the marketIncreases accepted without supportThe supplier's cost support and a matching index
High VPO value on one planEstimating or specification gapWhether the same item repeats across homes
Rebate-eligible spend below thresholdVolume spread across agreementsWhich agreement each division is buying under

Here is an illustrative example, not a customer result. A builder with six divisions finds the same sliding door bought at five different unit prices. Two of the prices reflect a larger size and a premium glass package. One reflects freight to a remote market. The remaining gap is one division buying on a local agreement while a lower national price exists. Only that last gap is a purchasing opportunity.

Rising prices are worth checking against a public index; our guide to responding to a supplier price increase letter lists producer price indexes for windows and doors. Turning findings into ongoing measures is covered in purchasing KPIs for homebuilder CFOs.

What gets in the way, and how do you start anyway?

The biggest obstacle is data spread across divisions and systems that were never designed to be combined. Divisions acquired with their own systems, cost codes and supplier agreements can keep them, and even divisions on the same system use it differently. Waiting for perfect data means never starting.

Hunter Caudle, co-founder and president of Vosik, on where he would begin:

“The first thing I would do is pull what I could, and then ask the other divisions for their input.”

Hunter Caudle, Co-Founder and President, Vosik

  1. Start with the divisions you can reach. Pull complete data from the largest divisions first; they usually hold most of the spend.
  2. Pick two or three categories. High-cost, high-volume categories such as windows and doors show results fastest.
  3. Request the rest in a fixed format. Send other divisions a short template with the fields you need, not an open request.
  4. Mark what is estimated. Flag any figure that came from a division's own summary rather than transaction data.
  5. Repeat on a schedule. A spend analysis run once is a snapshot. Run quarterly, it shows whether findings were fixed.

Frequently asked questions

What is spend analysis in homebuilding?

Spend analysis in homebuilding is a review of everything a builder bought over a period, organized by supplier, cost category, division and time, and compared with the agreements that should apply. It shows where the builder buys off contract, pays different prices for the same item, or could consolidate suppliers.

What is a spend cube?

A spend cube is spend data organized along several dimensions at once, such as supplier, category, division and period, so it can be viewed from any angle without rebuilding it. A pivot table is the simplest version. For a homebuilder, adding plan or community and contract status makes the cube most useful.

How do you classify homebuilder spend?

Map every division's purchases to one shared list of cost categories. The NAHB Chart of Accounts, which gives windows code 3300 and exterior doors code 4300, and CSI MasterFormat, which groups both in Division 08, Openings, are practical starting points. Consistency across divisions matters more than which standard is chosen.

How often should a homebuilder run a spend analysis?

A full spend analysis once a year, with quarterly updates for the largest categories, works for most large builders. Quarterly updates show whether off-contract buying and price gaps found earlier were actually fixed.

Can a homebuilder do a spend analysis in Excel?

A single-division builder can do a useful spend analysis in Excel with pivot tables. Across many divisions, systems and suppliers, the cleaning and mapping work has to be repeated every time new data arrives, which is where spreadsheets become hard to keep accurate.

What is the first step in a spend analysis?

The first step is to gather twelve months of purchase order and invoice data from the divisions you can reach, along with the supplier agreements that apply. Start with the largest divisions and two or three high-cost categories, then extend to the rest.

Keeping the analysis current across every division

The hard part of a spend analysis at a large builder is not the pivot table. It is gathering data from divisions on different systems, cleaning it, matching it to the right agreements, and then doing it again next quarter.
