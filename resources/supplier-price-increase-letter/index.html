The short version

Below is how I think about price increase letters, and how to handle one by hand.

A supplier price increase letter is a written request to change the agreed price of materials from a stated date. For a homebuilder it is a proposal to modify the supplier agreement, not a change the builder is required to accept. The right response is to check the letter against the agreement, ask for support, and answer in writing before the effective date.

“It always moved so fast that upon receiving a price increase letter that percent increase became the baseline, whether wrong, right, or indifferent. We would then negotiate low against an increase and meet somewhere in the middle with no evidence backing any of it. It became clear to me that our trust in suppliers and vendors was not the best way to protect the business.”

Key facts at a glance

  • Under UCC Section 2-209(1), a modification to a contract for goods needs no new consideration to be binding, so whether the builder agrees is what decides the change.
  • If a signed supplier agreement says changes must be made in a signed writing, UCC Section 2-209(2) generally holds both sides to that requirement.
  • A modification attempt that fails the signed-writing requirement can still operate as a waiver under UCC Section 2-209(4), which is why paying a new price without objecting carries risk.
  • When no price is fixed, UCC Section 2-305 sets a reasonable price at the time for delivery, and a price left to one party must be fixed in good faith.
  • The U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics publishes monthly producer price indexes for wood window and door manufacturing and for metal window and door manufacturing, free on FRED.
  • ConsensusDocs 200.1 is a published model for adjusting price to an agreed objective market index, and it can work in both directions when prices fall.
  • The U.S. International Trade Commission publishes the Harmonized Tariff Schedule, which lists tariff classifications and general duty rates by product.

What is a supplier price increase letter, and does a builder have to accept it?

A supplier price increase letter asks a homebuilder to pay more for materials from a stated effective date. For goods such as windows and doors, the letter is a proposed modification of the supplier agreement. Under UCC Section 2-209(1), a modification needs no new consideration to bind both sides, which puts the weight on whether the builder agrees to it.

Two parts of the same section matter to a purchasing team. If the signed agreement requires changes to be made in a signed writing, UCC Section 2-209(2) generally holds both parties to that clause. A modification that fails that test can still operate as a waiver under UCC Section 2-209(4), so a builder that pays the new invoices without objecting may weaken its own position.

Where an agreement leaves the price open, UCC Section 2-305 fills the gap with a reasonable price at the time for delivery and requires a price set by one party to be fixed in good faith. States adopt the UCC with their own variations, so this is general information, not legal advice. How it applies depends on your state and the agreement you signed.

What should a buyer check first when an increase letter arrives?

A homebuilder's buyer should check the increase letter against the supplier agreement before looking at the market. Six items decide most letters: the notice period, any price cap or price protection, the effective date against open purchase orders, which products are covered, the stated reason, and the arithmetic. The agreement answers the first four. The letter has to support the last two.

What to checkWhere to find itWhat a problem looks like
Notice periodSupplier agreement, pricing or term sectionLetter gives 30 days. Agreement requires 60.
Price cap or price protectionPricing schedule or amendmentsIncrease exceeds the agreed cap, or lands inside a protected period
Effective date vs open POsPurchasing systemNew price applied to orders already placed at the old price
Products coveredLetter vs item list in the agreementIncrease applied to items the letter never named
Stated reasonThe letter itself“Market conditions” with no index, data, or detail
The mathLetter and current invoicesPercentage applied to the wrong base, or rounded up

Here is how small details become real money. Say a window supplier asks for 8% more on a $250,000 order, effective in 30 days, and the agreement requires 60 days' notice. That is an extra $20,000, and the notice gap is a legitimate reason to question the effective date before paying it. This is an illustrative example, not a customer result. What documentation should a builder ask the supplier for?

A homebuilder should ask the supplier to support any increase in writing before accepting it. The request should cover which cost moved, by how much, over what period, and how that cost flows into the quoted price. A supplier with a real cost change can usually show it. A supplier testing the market often cannot.

  • The cost driver. Which input changed (glass, aluminum, vinyl, freight, labor, duty) and its share of the product's cost.
  • The period. The dates the cost change covers, so it can be compared with an index over the same months.
  • The index or data used, if the supplier cites one, with the series name so the buyer can check it.
  • The product list and old and new prices by item, not a blanket percentage.
  • The effect on open purchase orders, stated in writing.

How can a builder tell whether the increase matches the market?

A homebuilder can compare a supplier's requested increase with the U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics producer price index for the same product category over the same months. BLS publishes monthly indexes for wood window and door manufacturing and for metal window and door manufacturing, and both are free on FRED, the Federal Reserve Bank of St. Louis data site.

MaterialBLS index (FRED series ID)How to use it
Wood windows and doorsWood Window and Door Manufacturing (PCU321911321911)Compare the percent change over the supplier's stated period
Metal windows and doors, including sliding glass doorsMetal Window and Door Manufacturing (PCU332321332321)Same comparison for aluminum and steel products
Building materials overallNet Inputs to Residential Construction, Goods (WPUIP2311001)A check on broad claims about “market conditions”

An index is a reference point, not a price. It will not match one supplier's costs, freight, or product mix, and these series are not seasonally adjusted. A large gap between the letter and the index is a reason to ask questions, not proof the increase is wrong.

What if the supplier blames tariffs?

When a supplier cites tariffs, a homebuilder should ask which imported inputs are affected, their country of origin, their tariff classification, the duty rate applied, and what share of the product's cost those inputs represent. Published classifications and general duty rates are in the Harmonized Tariff Schedule maintained by the U.S. International Trade Commission.

A tariff on one imported component does not justify the same percentage increase on the whole window or door. Ask the supplier to show the duty's share of the finished product's cost, and whether the charge is a separate surcharge that falls away if the duty changes, or a permanent increase to the base price. Vosik is not a customs or legal tool. Questions about classification or legality belong with a customs broker or counsel.

“Oftentimes we didn't know what to ask them for. In reality we knew that in a window or a door material makeup, the BOM (bill of materials), there were only certain components that were impacted by tariffs, but we had no way of proving what portion or what that meant cost-wise.”

How should a builder negotiate the increase?

A homebuilder rarely has to choose between accepting an increase in full and rejecting it outright. The stronger responses change the terms: hold the agreed notice period, protect open orders, phase the increase in, cap it, or tie it to an index that also moves down. Which one fits depends on what the agreement says and how strong the supplier's support is.

ResponseWhen it fitsWhat to ask for
Hold the notice periodLetter gives less notice than the agreementEffective date moved to match the agreement
Protect open ordersIncrease would hit orders already placedOld price honored on all open POs
Phase it inCost change is real but suddenIncrease split across two or more dates
Cap itSupport covers part of the requestIncrease limited to the documented cost change
Tie it to an indexCosts are volatile in both directionsAdjustment to a named index, up and down
Decline itNo support, or it breaks a cap or protected periodWritten decline citing the agreement

For index-tied pricing, ConsensusDocs 200.1 is a useful published model. It adjusts price to an agreed objective market index and can work as a de-escalation clause when prices fall. It was written for construction contracts, but its logic carries over to supplier agreements.

What should a buyer never do when an increase letter arrives?

A homebuilder's buyer should never let a price increase take effect by default. The common ways that happens are paying the first invoice at the new price without a written response, updating the price in the purchasing system before the agreement has been checked, and letting the effective date pass while the letter sits in an inbox.

  • Do not pay new-price invoices until the letter has been answered in writing.
  • Do not update item prices in the purchasing system until the change is agreed.
  • Do not negotiate by phone without confirming the outcome in writing.
  • Do not accept a blanket percentage without an item-by-item price list.

The one thing a buyer should never do, in Caudle's words:

“Accept it blindly without at least pushing back.”

Hunter Caudle, Co-Founder and President, Vosik

How should the response be documented?

A homebuilder should answer every supplier price increase letter in writing, before the effective date, and keep the letter, the response, and the final agreed terms together with the supplier agreement. The record matters later, when finance asks what changed, and when the next letter arrives from the same supplier.

A short response template:

Thank you for your letter dated [date] regarding pricing for [products]. Under Section [x] of our agreement dated [date], price changes require [60] days' written notice and are subject to [the agreed cap]. Before we can consider the change, please send the cost support, the period it covers, and item-level old and new prices. Until the change is agreed in writing, all orders, including open purchase orders, will be invoiced at current contract pricing.

Track three numbers separately for every letter: the amount the supplier requested, the amount the builder questioned, and the amount actually avoided or recovered. Frequently asked questions

Can a supplier raise prices in the middle of a contract?

A supplier can ask to raise prices mid-contract, but for goods the increase is a modification the builder has to agree to. Under UCC Section 2-209, a modification needs no new consideration, and if the signed agreement requires changes in a signed writing, that requirement generally applies. Check the agreement's pricing, notice, and amendment clauses, and ask counsel about your state's rules.

How much notice should a supplier give before a price increase?

The notice a supplier must give is whatever the supplier agreement says, so the agreement's pricing and notice clauses are the first place to look. If the agreement sets a notice period in days, an increase that gives less notice is a reason to move the effective date. If the agreement is silent, negotiate a notice period at the next renewal.

What should I ask a supplier to justify a price increase?

Ask the supplier to name the cost that moved, its share of the product's cost, the period of the change, any index used, and item-level old and new prices. Then compare the request with the matching Bureau of Labor Statistics producer price index over the same months before deciding whether to accept, negotiate, or decline.

Is it better to reject a supplier price increase or negotiate it?

Negotiating is usually better than rejecting, because the strongest outcomes change the terms rather than the answer. A homebuilder can hold the notice period, protect open purchase orders, phase the increase in, cap it at the documented cost change, or tie future pricing to an index that also moves down. Decline outright when the increase has no support or breaks a cap.

How do I respond to a supplier price increase in writing?

Respond before the effective date. Acknowledge the letter, cite the agreement's notice and pricing terms, request cost support and item-level prices, and state that orders will be invoiced at current contract pricing until a change is agreed in writing. Keep the letter and response with the supplier agreement.

How do I know if a tariff surcharge is legitimate?

Ask the supplier which imported inputs the tariff applies to, their tariff classification and duty rate, their country of origin, and their share of the finished product's cost. Published classifications and general duty rates are in the U.S. International Trade Commission's Harmonized Tariff Schedule. Questions about classification or legality belong with a customs broker or counsel.
