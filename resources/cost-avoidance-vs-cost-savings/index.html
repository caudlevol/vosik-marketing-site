Below is how I think about cost avoidance and cost savings, and how to keep them straight by hand.

Cost savings is a reduction in what a homebuilder actually pays compared with a defined baseline, such as the last contract price. Cost avoidance is a price increase the builder resisted, so spending is lower than it would have been but not lower than before. Report them as separate numbers, and count a saving only when it shows up on invoices or in cash.

“Every quarter, purchasing reported savings. Every quarter, finance couldn't find them. The one that stuck with me: we negotiated a supplier price decrease and reported the annualized number as savings the day the letter was signed.”

Key facts at a glance

  • The Chartered Institute of Procurement and Supply (CIPS) defines a saving as a reduction in expenditure compared with a defined baseline.
  • CIPS defines cost avoidance as resistance to unbudgeted, unplanned or unexpected price increases, and states that cost avoidance is not a saving, though it should still be measured.
  • CIPS recommends that finance and procurement agree how savings are measured for key spending, and that savings be linked to the financial accounts where possible.
  • In the National Association of Home Builders 2024 Cost of Constructing a Home survey, construction costs accounted for 64.4% of the average price of a home.
  • Public homebuilders such as LGI Homes report that home construction costs are allocated to individual homes and expensed to cost of sales as homes are closed.
  • Because of that, a lower material price reaches a builder's income statement only as the homes built with that material close, not on the day the contract is signed.
  • A builder can avoid cost and still spend more than budget: negotiating a requested increase down is avoidance, while the smaller increase that remains is still new cost.

What is the difference between cost savings and cost avoidance?

Cost savings lowers what a homebuilder pays compared with a baseline it already had, such as last year's contract price for the same window. Cost avoidance stops a price from rising as far as a supplier wanted. Savings shrink spending. Avoidance keeps it from growing. Both are real work by a purchasing team, but only savings make spending smaller than it was.

CIPS draws the same line. Its position paper on savings defines a saving as a reduction in expenditure compared with a defined baseline and cost avoidance as resistance to unbudgeted, unplanned or unexpected price increases. It states plainly that cost avoidance is not a saving, and that it still belongs in how procurement performance is measured.

Why do purchasing savings disappear before they reach the P&L?

Purchasing savings disappear when they are counted at the moment of agreement instead of the moment of payment. A negotiated price is a promise. Between the signature and the cash, the new price has to reach the price sheet, the purchasing system and every invoice, and any rebate has to be claimed and paid. Each step is a place the saving can leak.

Here is where that saving went on my team:

“The decrease was supposed to land on the next price sheet and only partially did; nobody reconciled invoice by invoice. And a rebate program that was earned in the savings report was never actually claimed.”

Hunter Caudle, Co-Founder and President, Vosik

The common leaks at a homebuilder:

  • The new price never reaches the invoice. The agreement changes, but the price sheet, the item price in the purchasing system or the supplier's billing does not, so invoices keep coming at the old price.
  • Only some divisions get it. A national price is negotiated, but divisions keep buying on local agreements or off contract.
  • The forecast volume never happens. An annualized saving assumes a number of starts. If starts slow, the saving shrinks with them.
  • The rebate is earned but not claimed. Rebate programs have thresholds, reporting requirements and submission windows. A rebate that is never submitted is never paid.
  • The specification changes. A new window series or an upgraded door changes the price for reasons that have nothing to do with negotiation.

When does a material price change show up in a homebuilder's financials?

A material price change shows up in a homebuilder's income statement when the homes built with that material close. Builders carry construction costs in inventory, home by home, and move them to cost of sales at closing. LGI Homes' annual report, for example, states that home construction costs are allocated to the cost of individual homes and that inventory costs for completed homes are expensed to cost of sales as homes are closed.

This timing gap is one reason purchasing and finance disagree. Purchasing reports a lower window price when the agreement is signed. Finance sees it later, spread across the homes that use those windows, as they close, mixed in with every other change in cost per home. Unless both teams track the same homes and the same invoices, the number purchasing reported and the margin finance sees will not reconcile.

It is also why purchasing matters so much to margin. In NAHB's 2024 Cost of Constructing a Home survey, construction costs were 64.4% of the average sales price. Small changes in what a builder pays for materials move gross margin, but only once they land in the cost of homes that close.

How should a builder track savings from found to realized?

A homebuilder should track every purchasing result through three stages, and report each stage as its own number: found, agreed and realized. Money found is an opportunity. Money agreed is a promise from a supplier. Money realized is confirmed on invoices or received as cash. Only the last number belongs in a savings figure that finance will accept.

RealizedThe money has reached the builderInvoices billed at the new price, credits applied, rebate payment receivedFinance or accounts payable

The rule, put simply:

“The problem is that 'savings' got counted at the moment of the handshake instead of the moment of the cash.”

Realized savings should be measured on actual quantity, not forecast. If a sliding door price drops by $80 and the forecast was 1,500 units, the agreed figure is $120,000 a year. If 900 doors are invoiced at the new price and the rest at the old one, the realized figure is $72,000 until the overbilled invoices are credited. This is an illustrative example, not a customer result.

How do you calculate cost avoidance?

Cost avoidance is the difference between the price a supplier asked for and the price the builder agreed to, multiplied by the quantity actually bought at that price. The baseline is the supplier's written request, which is why the request needs to be kept with the agreement. Without it, there is nothing to measure the avoidance against.

LineAmountHow to report it
Annual window spend at current contract price$3,000,000Baseline
Supplier requests a 7% increase+$210,000Requested
Builder negotiates the increase down to 3%+$90,000Agreed new cost
Cost avoidance (requested minus agreed)$120,000Report as avoidance, not savings
Change in spend vs a flat-price budget+$90,000 over budgetReport as a cost increase

The example shows why avoidance and savings cannot share a line. Purchasing did good work and kept $120,000 off the cost of those windows, yet the builder still spends $90,000 more than a budget that assumed flat pricing. Adding the $120,000 to a savings total would tell the CFO that costs went down when they went up. This is an illustrative example, not a customer result.

Two refinements make avoidance figures more credible. Use actual quantities bought at the agreed price, not the full-year forecast. And keep only increases the supplier formally requested in writing. An increase someone expected but nobody asked for is not a reliable baseline.

What should a purchasing report to finance include?

A purchasing report to a homebuilder's finance team should show realized savings, agreed savings not yet realized, and cost avoidance as three separate figures, by division and by supplier, with the evidence behind each one. CIPS recommends that finance and procurement agree the measurement method for key spending, and the start of the year is the time to do it.

  • Realized savings, reconciled to invoices, credits and rebate payments. This is the only figure that should be described as savings.
  • Agreed but not yet realized, with the expected date each item should appear on invoices or be paid.
  • Cost avoidance, shown separately, with each supplier's written request as the baseline.
  • Rebates in three columns: earned on paper, submitted, and paid.
  • Leakage, meaning agreed prices that did not reach invoices, and what is being done to recover them.
  • Price increases accepted, so finance sees cost moving in both directions, not just the wins.

Agree the definitions with finance before reporting, not after. When both teams use the same baseline and the same evidence, the purchasing report stops being a debate and becomes a forecast finance can use. See how Vosik organizes purchasing data.

What mistakes inflate reported purchasing savings?

Most inflated savings figures come from counting too early, counting the same dollar twice, or calling something a saving that is not one. None of these require bad intent. They come from reporting on agreements rather than on invoices and cash.

  • Annualizing a price decrease the day it is signed, before any invoice reflects it.
  • Adding cost avoidance to the savings total.
  • Counting a rebate when the threshold is met rather than when the payment arrives.
  • Reporting the same national agreement as savings in corporate and in every division.
  • Measuring against a supplier's list price instead of what the builder actually paid.
  • Ignoring volume, so a saving per unit is multiplied by starts that never happened.
  • Leaving out increases the builder accepted, so the report shows only one direction.

Frequently asked questions

Is cost avoidance a real saving?

Cost avoidance is real value, but it is not a saving. It keeps spending from rising as far as a supplier wanted, while spending still goes up. CIPS states that cost avoidance is not a saving and should still be measured as part of procurement performance. Report it, but on its own line.

What is the difference between hard savings and soft savings?

Hard savings are reductions in what a business actually pays compared with a baseline, and they appear in the financial statements. Soft savings are benefits that do not reduce spending, such as cost avoidance or process improvements. For a homebuilder, hard savings appear as lower cost of sales on homes that close. Soft savings do not.

How do you calculate cost avoidance?

Take the price the supplier requested in writing, subtract the price the builder agreed to, and multiply by the quantity actually bought at that price. If a window supplier asked for 7% on $3 million of annual spend and agreed to 3%, cost avoidance is $120,000, and spending still rose by $90,000.

When do purchasing savings show up in a homebuilder's financial statements?

Purchasing savings show up when the homes built with the lower-cost materials close. Homebuilders carry construction costs in inventory home by home and move them to cost of sales at closing, so a price change signed today reaches gross margin later, as those homes close.

Should rebates count as purchasing savings?

Rebates should count as realized savings only when they are paid, not when the volume threshold is reached. Report rebates earned, submitted and paid as separate figures, so a missed submission deadline is visible. How a rebate is recorded in the accounts is a question for the builder's controller or CPA.

Who should own the purchasing savings number?

Purchasing should own the found and agreed figures, and finance should confirm the realized figure from invoices, credits and payments. CIPS recommends that finance and procurement agree the measurement method together. Splitting ownership this way keeps both teams honest and makes the number one the CFO will use.
