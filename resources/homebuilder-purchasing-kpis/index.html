Purchasing KPIs for Homebuilders: Which Numbers Should a CFO Track?

By Hunter Caudle, Co-Founder and President · November 19, 2026

The short version

Gross margin tells a CFO that something changed. Purchasing KPIs tell the CFO what changed, in which division and with which supplier. Vosik gives purchasing and finance one set of numbers, by division and by supplier.

A homebuilder CFO should track purchasing KPIs that tie directly to margin per closed home: material cost per home by plan and division, the share of spend bought on contract, invoice prices against contract prices, price increases requested and accepted, realized savings, rebates paid, and variance purchase orders per home. Each should be reported by division, with the evidence behind it.

These are the numbers a builder's CFO actually watches:

“Cost and profit per home. Good purchasing directly impacts margin per home, so a CFO watches margin and pre-tax profit by division.”

Hunter Caudle, Co-Founder and President, Vosik

Key facts at a glance

  • In the National Association of Home Builders Cost of Doing Business Study, builders reported an average gross profit margin of 20.7% for 2023.
  • The same study puts builders' cost of sales, which covers land and direct and indirect construction costs, at 79.3% of revenue.
  • NAHB reports an average net profit margin of 8.7% for 2023, so a change in material cost moves net profit by the same dollars on a much smaller base.
  • PulteGroup reports in its annual filing that it uses the specific identification method to accumulate home construction costs, which means costs are tracked home by home.
  • Because builders move those costs to cost of sales as homes close, a purchasing KPI that is not tied to closed homes will not reconcile with the margin a CFO sees.
  • The Chartered Institute of Procurement and Supply (CIPS) measures a saving against a defined baseline and treats cost avoidance as separate from savings.
  • CIPS recommends that finance and procurement agree how savings are measured for key spending, which applies to every KPI in this article.

Why should a homebuilder CFO track purchasing KPIs at all?

A homebuilder CFO should track purchasing KPIs because cost of sales is most of a builder's revenue. In NAHB's Cost of Doing Business Study, builders' cost of sales, covering land and direct and indirect construction costs, was 79.3% of revenue in 2023, leaving an average gross margin of 20.7% and a net margin of 8.7%.

At those margins, a small change in what a builder pays for windows, doors or any other major material category moves profit per home. Gross margin tells a CFO that something changed. Purchasing KPIs tell the CFO what changed, in which division, with which supplier, and whether anyone can do something about it.

Which purchasing KPIs should a homebuilder CFO track?

A homebuilder CFO should track a short list of purchasing KPIs that each connect to margin per closed home and each have a clear owner. Ten cover most of what matters. Track them by division and by major supplier, and only add more when a decision depends on them.

How should material cost per home be measured?

Material cost per home should be measured on closed homes, by plan and division, so it lines up with the cost of sales the CFO already reports. Builders carry construction costs in inventory and move them to cost of sales at closing. PulteGroup's annual report, for example, states that it uses the specific identification method to accumulate home construction costs, so the cost of each home follows that home to closing.

Compare like with like. A division building larger plans or more upgraded homes will show a higher cost per home for reasons that have nothing to do with purchasing. Track the same plan over time, and split changes into three parts: price (what was paid per item), quantity (how much was used), and mix (which plans and options were built).

Change in cost per homeUsual causeWho owns it
PriceSupplier increases, off-contract buying, invoices above contractPurchasing
QuantityWaste, rework, VPOs, estimating errorsConstruction and estimating
MixLarger plans, more options, different communitiesSales and product

How do you measure contract compliance at a homebuilder?

Contract compliance at a homebuilder is measured two ways: how much spend goes through a supplier agreement at all, and whether invoices under that agreement are billed at the agreed price. The first catches buying outside negotiated terms. The second catches agreed prices that never reached the invoice.

  • On-contract spend. Divide spend on items covered by an agreement by total spend in the same category. Low figures point to divisions or communities buying outside agreements.
  • Invoice price compliance. Compare invoice unit prices with the contract price for the same item and date. Report the dollar value billed above contract, not just a percentage of lines.
  • Recovery. Track how much of the overbilled amount has been credited back. Found is not the same as recovered.

Both measures depend on knowing which agreement applies, which is harder than it sounds at a builder that has grown by acquisition or runs divisions on different systems. That is the reporting problem behind every KPI:

“In order to put a report together, it takes hours if not days to consolidate, and even after that there is often data manipulation or guessing involved.”

Why track variance purchase orders?

Variance purchase orders, or VPOs, are purchase orders issued for work or materials beyond the original job budget for a home: rework, missed items, extras and corrections. Tracking VPOs per home, by plan and by commodity, shows a CFO where homes cost more than their budget and why. Repeated VPOs for the same item on the same plan usually point to an estimating or specification problem, not a one-off.

Here is what the data showed me:

“VPO data was eye-opening, because there is such a large volume, it is nearly impossible to manage via Excel.”

Report three things: VPO count and value per closed home, the top plans and commodities by VPO value, and the value of VPOs that should have been charged back to a trade or supplier but were not.

Where can purchasing KPIs mislead a CFO?

Purchasing KPIs mislead a CFO when they measure the wrong baseline, mix price with quantity or mix, or count results before the money arrives. Each KPI in the list above has a common way to go wrong.

KPIHow it misleadsThe fix
Material cost per homePlan and option mix changes look like price changesCompare the same plan; split price, quantity and mix
On-contract spendA stale contract price makes compliance look good while the builder overpaysReview contract prices at renewal against the market
Reported savingsCounted at signing, annualized on forecast volumeReport realized savings only, on actual volume
Cost avoidanceAdded to savings, so cost looks lower when it roseReport it on its own line
RebatesCounted when the threshold is met, not when paidReport earned, submitted and paid separately
Price spreadLegitimate differences (freight, spec, quantity, timing) look like overpayingInvestigate the gap before assuming a problem

CIPS recommends that finance and procurement agree how savings are measured for key spending. The same rule works for every KPI here: agree the definition, the baseline and the data source with finance before the first report, not after the first disagreement.

How should purchasing KPIs be reported?

Purchasing KPIs should be reported monthly, by division, on the same closed-home basis finance uses for margin, with the evidence behind each figure available on request. A one-page summary for the CFO, with detail by division and supplier behind it, works better than a long dashboard nobody reads.

  1. Start with cost per closed home, by division, split into price, quantity and mix.
  2. Show supplier pressure: increases requested, accepted and avoided this month and year to date.
  3. Show results in both directions: realized savings, price increases accepted, and overbilling found and recovered.
  4. Show money still due: rebates earned but not paid, credits agreed but not applied, and VPOs not yet charged back.
  5. Show what is coming: agreements up for renewal and increase letters with effective dates in the next quarter.

Frequently asked questions

What are the most important purchasing KPIs for a homebuilder?

The most important purchasing KPIs for a homebuilder are material cost per closed home by plan and division, on-contract spend, invoice price compliance, price increases requested and accepted, realized savings, rebates paid, and variance purchase orders per home. Each connects directly to margin per home.

How do you measure purchasing performance at a homebuilder?

Measure purchasing performance on closed homes, by division, against baselines agreed with finance. Separate price from quantity and mix in cost per home, report realized savings apart from cost avoidance, and track how much negotiated pricing actually reaches invoices.

What is a VPO in homebuilding?

A VPO, or variance purchase order, is a purchase order issued for work or materials beyond a home's original job budget, such as rework, missed items or extras. Tracking VPOs per home by plan and commodity shows where homes run over budget and whether the cause repeats.

What share of a homebuilder's revenue goes to cost of sales?

In NAHB's Cost of Doing Business Study, builders' cost of sales, which covers land and direct and indirect construction costs, averaged 79.3% of revenue in 2023. That left an average gross profit margin of 20.7% and a net profit margin of 8.7%.

How often should a CFO review purchasing KPIs?

Monthly is a practical cadence for most builders, on the same closed-home basis as the margin report. Price increase letters and agreement renewals need attention as they arrive, because their deadlines do not wait for the monthly review.

Should purchasing KPIs be tracked by division?

Yes. Company-wide totals hide the differences that matter, such as one division buying off contract or paying more for the same item. Report each KPI by division and roll it up, so the CFO sees both the total and where it comes from.
