Price Increase Notice Periods: What Should a Homebuilder's Supplier Agreement Say?

By Hunter Caudle, Co-Founder and President · November 16, 2026

The short version

Most builders argue about the size of a price increase. The agreement should already have settled the timing. Vosik puts each supplier's notice period, cap and terms in front of your buyer the day an increase letter arrives.

A homebuilder's supplier agreement should state how many days' written notice a supplier must give before a price increase, that the new price applies only to orders placed after the effective date, which open orders and sold homes are price protected, and how much prices can rise in a set period. Without those terms, the builder has little to hold a supplier to when an increase letter arrives.

There is a gap between what builders want and what they get:

“Notice periods of 90 days would be great, but oftentimes they are shorter, like 30 or 60 days.”

Hunter Caudle, Co-Founder and President, Vosik

Key facts at a glance

  • Under UCC Section 2-309(1), when a contract for goods does not set a time for an action, the time allowed is a reasonable time, which leaves the question open to dispute.
  • Under UCC Section 2-309(3), ending an ongoing supply contract, except on an agreed event, requires reasonable notification to the other party.
  • Under UCC Section 2-207(2), between merchants, extra terms in an order acknowledgment or confirmation can become part of the contract unless the order limits acceptance to its own terms, the terms materially alter it, or the buyer objects.
  • Under UCC Section 2-209(2), a signed agreement that requires changes to be made in a signed writing generally holds both parties to that requirement.
  • The federal government's standard economic price adjustment clause for supplies, FAR 52.216-2, caps total increases at 10% of the original unit price and passes decreases through by the same percentage.
  • The same FAR clause states that an increase does not apply to quantities scheduled for delivery before its effective date.
  • PulteGroup reports in its annual filing that it is generally unable to pass construction cost increases to customers who have already signed, because sales contracts generally fix the home price at signing.

Why does the notice period matter more to a homebuilder?

A notice period matters more to a homebuilder than to most buyers because many of its homes are already sold at a fixed price when materials are bought. PulteGroup's annual report states that the company is generally unable to pass construction cost increases to customers who have already entered into sales contracts, because those contracts generally fix the price of the home when the contract is signed, which may be well in advance of construction.

That means a supplier increase on windows or sliding doors lands directly on the margin of every sold home that has not yet received them. The notice period is the builder's time to check the increase, push back, adjust pricing on homes not yet sold, and re-bid if it has to. A short notice period shrinks all of that, and a missing one removes it.

The cost of leaving it vague is simple:

“If the terms aren't clearly defined, you risk margin erosion.”

Hunter Caudle, Co-Founder and President, Vosik

The notice period also decides how much room a buyer has when the letter arrives. Our guide to responding to a supplier price increase letter covers what to check and how to answer. This article covers the terms to put in the agreement before any letter is sent.

How long should the notice period be?

A homebuilder should set the notice period by its own sales and build cycle, not by what a supplier proposes. The question is how long it takes, from the day a price changes, to stop selling homes at prices that assumed the old cost, and to finish the homes already sold. The longer that cycle, the more notice the builder needs.

Notice periodWhat it gives the builderWhere it falls short
30 daysTime to check the letter and respond in writingLittle time to re-price homes for sale or re-bid; sold homes absorb the increase
60 daysTime to check, negotiate, and update pricing on homes not yet soldHomes sold before the change may still be built at the new price
90 days or moreTime to negotiate, re-price, and re-bid if neededSuppliers may ask for something in return, such as volume or term commitments

These are trade-offs, not a standard. Longer notice is worth more on products with long lead times, high cost per home, or few alternative suppliers, such as windows and sliding doors. Expect suppliers to resist long notice when their own costs are volatile; an index-based adjustment, covered below, is one way to give them flexibility without giving up notice.

What should the notice clause actually say?

A notice clause should say more than a number of days. It should set how notice is given and to whom, what it must contain, when the new price takes effect, which orders it applies to, and how often prices can change. Each missing detail is an argument the supplier can make later.

TermWhat to write inWhy it matters
LengthA set number of days' written notice before any price changeGives the builder time to check, negotiate and re-price
Form and recipientWritten notice to a named role, such as the national purchasing director, at a stated addressStops notice to a field contact or a line on an invoice from starting the clock
ContentsItems affected, old and new prices by item, effective date, and the cost support for the changeLets the builder check the increase instead of a blanket percentage
Start of the clockThe notice period runs from the day the builder receives the noticeAvoids disputes over the date a letter was written or sent
Which ordersNew prices apply only to purchase orders issued after the effective dateKeeps the new price off orders already placed
FrequencyNo more than a set number of price changes per yearPrevents a series of small increases, each with fresh notice
DecreasesPrice decreases pass through on the same termsMakes the clause work in both directions

Here is an illustrative sample to adapt with counsel, not a clause to copy as is:

Supplier may change prices no more than [twice] in any calendar year, by written notice to Buyer's [National Purchasing Director] at least [90] days before the effective date. Notice must list each affected item, its current and proposed price, and the cost basis for the change. New prices apply only to purchase orders issued on or after the effective date. Price decreases take effect on the same terms. No price change is binding unless agreed in a writing signed by both parties.

Illustrative sample only. Have counsel adapt it.

The last sentence connects to UCC Section 2-209(2), under which a signed agreement that requires changes in a signed writing generally holds both sides to it. It is what makes an unanswered price letter something other than an agreed change. State law varies, so have counsel confirm how this works where you buy.

What is price protection, and what should it cover?

Price protection is a promise that certain orders or homes will be billed at the old price even after an increase takes effect. For a homebuilder, it should cover open purchase orders, deliveries already scheduled, and ideally the materials for homes already under a sales contract. The federal model clause, FAR 52.216-2, takes a similar approach: an increase does not apply to quantities scheduled for delivery before its effective date.

  • Open purchase orders. Every PO issued before the effective date is billed at the price on the PO.
  • Scheduled deliveries. Releases already scheduled against a blanket order keep the old price, even if they ship after the effective date.
  • Sold homes. Materials for homes under a signed sales contract, or for starts released before the effective date, keep the old price. This is the hardest term to win and the most valuable.
  • Supplier delays. If the supplier ships late for its own reasons, the old price still applies to that order.

Price protection for sold homes is often negotiated as a list of lots or start dates the supplier receives with the notice. The builder gives visibility into its backlog; the supplier gives a defined window at the old price. Both sides know exactly what is covered.

Should the agreement cap price increases?

A supplier agreement should cap price increases, either per change, per year, or over the life of the agreement, so a builder's exposure has a ceiling it can budget for. FAR 52.216-2 is one published example: it caps the total of all increases at 10% of the original unit price, and it requires unit prices to fall by the same percentage when the supplier's established price falls.

A cap does not need to copy that number. What matters is that the agreement states one, says what happens above it (the builder may re-bid or terminate without penalty, for example), and treats decreases the same way as increases. A cap that only works upward invites increases right up to the cap.

Cap structureHow it worksBest fit
Per changeEach increase limited to a set percentageProducts with frequent, small cost changes
Per yearAll increases in a year limited to a set totalAgreements with set budget periods
Over the termTotal increases limited across the agreement, as in FAR 52.216-2Multi-year national agreements
Index-tiedPrices move with a named public index, up and down, within a bandVolatile inputs such as aluminum or glass

What about "prices subject to change" on supplier paperwork?

Order acknowledgments, quotes and invoices can carry lines such as "prices subject to change without notice." Under UCC Section 2-207(2), between merchants, extra terms in an acceptance or written confirmation can become part of the contract unless the buyer's order limits acceptance to its own terms, the new terms materially alter the deal, or the buyer objects within a reasonable time.

A homebuilder can close that gap with three steps:

  1. Put an order of precedence clause in the supplier agreement. State that the agreement controls over any quote, acknowledgment, invoice or website terms.
  2. Limit acceptance on every purchase order. The PO should say it is governed by the supplier agreement and that additional or different terms are rejected.
  3. Object in writing when new terms appear. If an acknowledgment adds a price-change term, reply that it is rejected and the agreement controls.

Whether a given term "materially alters" a contract is a legal question that depends on the facts and the state. Treat these steps as general information and ask counsel to review your PO and agreement language.

What if the agreement says nothing about notice?

If a supplier agreement says nothing about notice, the builder is left with general rules that are open to argument. Under UCC Section 2-309(1), a time not set in the contract is a reasonable time, and under Section 2-309(3), ending an ongoing supply contract requires reasonable notification. Neither tells a buyer how many days a supplier owes before raising prices.

The fix is to add the terms at the next renewal, amendment or new agreement, and to treat any price increase in the meantime as a proposal to negotiate, not a change already in effect. The first problem, though, is usually finding the agreement at all. That was my experience:

“Trying to find a contract, or even knowing there is a contract to reference, was always a major pain point.”

Hunter Caudle, Co-Founder and President, Vosik

How do buyers make sure these terms get used?

A notice clause only protects a homebuilder if the buyer who receives the increase letter knows it exists. At a builder with many suppliers and several divisions, agreements are signed by different people, amended over years and stored in different places. The terms that matter on the day a letter arrives are often the hardest to find.

  • Keep a one-page term summary for every supplier agreement: notice period, how notice must be given, price protection, cap, decrease terms, expiry and renewal dates.
  • Store agreements and amendments in one place that every division's buyers can search, not in individual inboxes.
  • Log every increase letter against the agreement on the day it arrives, with the notice date and the date the clock runs out.
  • Track renewals, so missing notice and cap terms are added before the agreement rolls over.
  • Report requested, agreed and avoided amounts separately, as covered in our guide to cost avoidance vs cost savings.

Frequently asked questions

Is 30 days enough notice for a supplier price increase?

Thirty days is usually enough time to check a price increase letter and respond in writing, but rarely enough to re-price homes for sale or re-bid a product. For high-cost, long-lead items such as windows and sliding doors, longer notice is worth negotiating. The right length depends on how long it takes the builder to sell and build homes priced on the old cost.

What is a price protection clause in a supplier agreement?

A price protection clause keeps certain orders at the old price after an increase takes effect. For a homebuilder, it typically covers open purchase orders and scheduled deliveries, and, when it can be negotiated, materials for homes already under a sales contract. It gives the builder a defined window to absorb or pass on a change.

Does "prices subject to change without notice" on an invoice override our supplier agreement?

Not necessarily. Under UCC Section 2-207, extra terms on an acknowledgment or confirmation can become part of a contract between merchants, but not if the buyer's order limits acceptance to its own terms, the terms materially alter the deal, or the buyer objects. An order of precedence clause and clear purchase order terms help keep the agreement in control.

Can a supplier raise the price on purchase orders already issued?

A supplier agreement should say that new prices apply only to purchase orders issued after the effective date, which keeps the old price on orders already placed. The federal model clause FAR 52.216-2 takes a similar approach for scheduled deliveries. If the agreement is silent, treat any change to an open order as a proposal to negotiate, and confirm the outcome in writing.

How often should a supplier be allowed to change prices?

A supplier agreement should limit price changes to a set number per year, such as once or twice, each with full written notice. Without a frequency limit, a supplier can send a series of smaller increases, each with its own notice period, that add up to more than the builder would have accepted at once.

What should a builder do if its supplier agreement has no notice period?

Treat any price increase as a proposal to negotiate, respond in writing before the stated effective date, and add notice, price protection and cap terms at the next renewal or amendment. Without a written term, the builder is left with general rules such as a "reasonable time" under UCC Section 2-309, which are open to argument.
